Four days ago, 24 November, OpenAI posted a 111-page preprint with a claim that, if it holds, closes a question open since 1969: the matrix multiplication exponent is exactly 9/4 — 2.25.

That number, ω, is the smallest exponent such that two n × n matrices can be multiplied in O(n^ω) operations. The schoolbook method needs n³. Strassen brought it down to about 2.81 in 1969, and decades of refinements carried the best published upper bound to ω < 2.371339. For decades the hope was ω = 2 — almost no cost beyond reading the n² output entries. This preprint puts the answer at 9/4: a quarter above the output size, and no lower.

But as always, I don't stop at the headline number. Half of this claim is not news. The upper bound ω ≤ 9/4 has been known since 2 October 2026, from OpenAI's own preprint — and since then several groups have rewritten the proof in their own ways: Rafael Pass with an explicit constructive proof, and the Amsterdam team with an exposition that holds over every field. The upper half has already been digested by humans.

What is new is the matching lower bound. Corollary 1.2 of the preprint states that any formal circuit needs at least ⌈n^(9/4)/2⌉ multiplication or division gates — even if every addition and multiplication by a constant is counted as free. For n = 1024, that means at least 2,965,821 gates, against 1,073,741,824 for the naive method.

And this is where I hold back. The README of the openai/math repository itself says the manuscripts were "produced by an unnamed internal OpenAI model" and should be understood as "raw research data" — some unformalized results may be problematic. Its Lean formalization covers only the division-free program, narrower than the paper's model, which allows division gates. As of Wednesday night, 25 November, there was no named expert review of the lower-bound proof. Emanuele Viola welcomed the result — "we now have TIGHT (non-trivial) bounds" — without reviewing the proof.

For the code I write day to day, nothing changes tonight. Algorithms near this exponent are asymptotic constructions; Pass himself calls the constants "galactic". An independent experiment with a single-threaded C implementation measured an effective exponent of 2.99 for naive multiplication and 2.79 for Strassen — close to their respective theories, and far from 2.25. GPU libraries still use cubic methods because they parallelize well. If the claim holds, what changes is the target: the search for ω = 2 ends in this model, and the search moves to constants that are practical.

This preprint did not arrive alone either. The nine manuscripts OpenAI posted on 24 November include a strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming and a disproof of the Hirsch conjecture — and in the same week, Anthropic and OpenAI each posted claimed proofs that VP differs from VNP, plus a deterministic linear-time algorithm for the minimum spanning tree whose idea was written as "fully generated by AI, specifically 6.2 Astra".

The pattern is clear, and this is the most interesting part of the week: machines now produce mathematics at a scale humans cannot follow one proof at a time. The bottleneck is no longer finding proofs, but checking them. I am waiting for a human review of that 9/4 lower bound. Until it arrives, this number is the most interesting claim of the week, not a finished theorem.
